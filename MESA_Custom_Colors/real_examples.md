## 1. Thermal Pulse on the AGB

**Real star:** **T-Ursae-Minoris (T-UMi)** – a Mira‑type long‑period variable currently undergoing a thermal pulse.

* **Period and amplitude evolution:** Long‑term AAVSO monitoring shows that around 1970 T-UMi’s Mira‑like pulsation began to change dramatically. Its 220‑day period shortened rapidly (~3.8daysyr⁻¹) and the light‑curve amplitude dropped; this behaviour is interpreted as the star entering a helium-shell thermal pulse.
* **Brightness range:** T-UMi oscillates between about *V*≈7.8 and 15.0 magnitudes, showing the large optical excursions expected during a thermal pulse.
* **Comparison with simulation:** Your synthetic TP‑AGB loop (Fig. 2) predicts decreases in brightness and colour during a pulse. T-UMi’s measured period shortening and amplitude fade match the model’s shrinking radius and luminosity.

**Other example:** **R-Sculptoris** – ALMA imaging of this carbon star reveals a detached dust shell and spiral wind. The shell was produced by a thermal pulse ~1800-years ago that lasted about 200-years, consistent with the short‑lived pulses predicted for AGB stars.

## 2. RRLyrae Radial Pulsators

**Real system:** **First-overtone RR-Lyrae stars in the MACHO survey** (Large Magellanic Cloud).

* **Band‑dependent amplitudes:** Fourier analysis of 785 overtone RR-Lyrae variables in V and R bands found that the R‑band pulsation amplitudes are about **20-% smaller** than the V‑band amplitudes.  This demonstrates the **amplitude ratio** used in your B–I synthetic light‑curve.
* **Large optical amplitudes:** In globular cluster M3, RR-Lyrae stars often exhibit V‑band amplitudes up to ~1-mag and even larger amplitudes in the B band, confirming that the amplitude increases toward bluer wavelengths as modelled.
* **Comparison with simulation:** Your model shows a 0.6‑mag amplitude in *I* and ~1.2‑mag amplitude in *B*. Real RR-Lyrae stars display similar band ratios and phase offsets.

## 3. Starspot‑Induced Variability

**Real stars:** **HK-Lacertae (RS-CVn binary)** & **rotational variables such as S-CrA.**

* **Short‑term variability:** UBV photometry of HK-Lac shows micro‑variability up to **0.03-mag in the U-band** on 20‑30‑min timescales.
* **Long‑term colour changes:** Over longer cycles the (U‑B) colour varies by **0.08–0.10-mag**, while (B‑V) remains constant.  These colour excursions arise from large starspots rotating in and out of view.
* **Small amplitudes:** Most rotating spotted variables show brightness changes of only a few hundredths of a magnitude; for example, the T-Tauri star S-CrA has a rotational period of 6.06-days and an amplitude of **0.05-mag**.  BY-Draconis stars, another class of spotted late‑type variables, usually vary by **≤0.5-mag**.
* **Comparison with simulation:** Your synthetic starspot model predicts blue‑weighted dimming and small colour loops. HK-Lac’s larger U‑band variability and negligible B‑V changes match these expectations, while the low amplitudes of S-CrA and BY-Dra stars demonstrate the subtle photometric signals that LSST will detect.

## 4. White Dwarf Cooling Sequence

**Observed phenomenon:** **Crystallisation‐induced pile‑up in Gaia H‑R diagrams**

* **Gaia discovery:** Gaia data show a **pile‑up of white dwarfs** at specific colours and luminosities on the Hertzsprung–Russell diagram.  This accumulation occurs because the release of latent heat when the core crystallises slows the cooling, making white dwarfs appear up to **2-billion years younger** than expected.  The observed pile‑up matches the kink in your synthetic white‑dwarf cooling track.
* **Recent update (2024):** New Gaia‑based research reported white dwarfs whose cooling halts for **>8Gyr** due to buoyant carbon‑oxygen crystals releasing gravitational energy.  The study found that these “floating crystals” release enough extra energy to interrupt cooling, challenging the textbook picture of monotonically cooling white dwarfs.
* **Comparison with simulation:** Your cooling‑sequence model traces the gradual fading of a 0.54-M☉ white dwarf. Gaia’s observed pile‑up and the newly discovered cooling hiatus both provide real data to validate the slowing and shape of the cooling curve.

## 5. Blue‑Loop Cepheid Evolution

**Real stars:** **Polaris** and **HD-344787** – Cepheid variables undergoing rapid period changes.

* **Polaris:** This nearby Cepheid exhibits a **period increase of 4.4–4.9-syr⁻¹** and a pulsation amplitude that declined from ~0.1-mag early in the 20th-century to just a few hundredths of a magnitude by the early 2000s.  Such rapid period increase and declining amplitude are consistent with **first‑crossing evolution through the instability strip** during a helium‑burning blue loop.
* **HD-344787:** Identified as a Polaris analogue, this Cepheid shows an even faster positive period change of **12.96±2.41-syr⁻¹** and a light‑curve amplitude that decreased from about **0.05-mag** to nearly zero by 2008–2009.  Evolutionary models predict that such rapid period increase and quenching amplitude occur during the **first blue‑loop crossing**.
* **Comparison with simulation:** Your blue‑loop model (5-M⊙ star) shows multiple crossings of the instability strip with large colour excursions.  Observations of Polaris and HD-344787 provide real examples of Cepheids experiencing rapid period changes and amplitude variations as they evolve through a blue loop.
